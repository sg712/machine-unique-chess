What I found in the positions

Shivansh Gupta · Analysis updated 8 September 2026

I used Stockfish and Maia to look for strong moves that a model of human play rarely chooses. That gave me a collection of positions to study, and eventually this trainer.

Here are the examples, the selection method, and the checks that changed my interpretation. I don’t yet know whether practicing these positions improves play in new ones; that needs a controlled study.

What “machine unique” means here

I use “machine unique” as shorthand for this filter. It doesn’t mean the move is impossible for a person to find. A position qualifies when Stockfish 17.1 at depth 16 gives its chosen move an evaluation at least 100 centipawns above Maia-2's favourite at 2000, and Maia-2 assigns the engine move at most 5% probability at each of 1100, 1400, 1700, and 2000.

This filter selected {{ '{:,}'.format(r.mu_n) }} of {{ '{:,}'.format(r.n) }} positions ({{ '%.2f'|format(r.mu_rate * 100) }}%) across nine mining batches. Sources include Lichess rated-game archives and the Lichess Elite Database, sampled at intervals during games. The corpus balances rating bands, but differs across batches in dates and time controls. It is not a random sample of all chess play. Ratings refer to the source Lichess pools; I haven’t converted them to FIDE ratings.

A sampling mistake I found: all 123,405 positions in this original corpus are Black to move. Sampling every fourth ply before a move always landed on Black’s turn. I’ve fixed the samplers and kept the old results identified as version 1. The new run below includes both sides; the original figures and trainer still describe the earlier collection.

Checking Maia-3 at higher ratings

Maia-3 ranked moves on 77 selected positions and 56 controls at five rating settings. On the selected positions, the engine move's top-five inclusion rose from 28.6% at 1100 to 57.1% at 2600; top-one agreement at 2600 was 2.6%. This smaller ranking experiment did not re-filter the full corpus using probabilities through 2600.

Higher top-five inclusion suggests the model ranks the move more favourably at higher ratings. It does not reveal whether a person considered the move and rejected it.

Many of the moves are quiet

Among positions meeting the same minimum 100-centipawn gap, the low-probability group has more quiet moves and fewer captures or checks. Thirty binary chess features distinguish the low- and high-probability groups at AUC {{ '%.3f'|format(contrast.auc_logistic) }} (five folds grouped by game; 7,853 positions in the two extreme groups). AUC measures ranking discrimination: 0.5 is chance-level ranking and 1 is perfect ranking.

The groups share a minimum gap, not identical positions or stakes. They were not matched on rating, phase, time control, or batch. The classifier predicts a model-defined label; it does not establish why humans miss moves. The material-exposure proxy is not a measurement of “no compensation.” A quiet move can begin a tactical combination.

Eight groups for practice

I grouped the first 1,745 positions using their layer-10 representations in Leela Chess Zero, then assigned the remaining 3,410 to the nearest group centre. That covers all 5,155 selected positions. I kept eight groups as a manageable way to organise practice, not because the analysis had discovered eight new chess concepts.

Inspect the grouping and motif evidence

At eight groups, silhouette is {{ '%.4f'|format(v.silhouette_real['8']) }} against {{ '%.4f'|format(v.silhouette_null['8']) }} for independently shuffled embedding dimensions. Absolute separation is small. This null destroys correlations as well as cluster structure, so the comparison alone does not establish meaningful chess categories.

Six groups are more stable under resampling than eight. Other clustering methods give different partitions and many noise points. The k=8 permutation result, p = {{ '%.3f'|format(v.external_validity['8'].p_value) }}, describes how often the null procedure produced a statistic at least as extreme as observed. It is exploratory across several choices of k, without a multiple-comparison correction.

On an earlier 646-position subset and 400 controls, twelve motif directions reconstructed the average selected-versus-control direction with R² = 0.46. Per-position reconstruction was similar for selected positions and controls (means 0.20 and 0.18). The unexplained component is residual to this particular basis; it is not proof of knowledge beyond chess vocabulary.

Checking the results

Some answers change at greater depth

I reanalysed eight positions per rating band with Stockfish 17.1, using a seeded random sample from {{ r.engine.n }} different games. All {{ r.engine.complete_n }} reached depth 20 in every search. The original move remained first in {{ r.engine.same_top_n }}/{{ r.engine.n }}.

Of {{ r.engine.numeric_n }} positions without mate scores, the original move still beat Maia-2's 2000 favourite by at least 100 centipawns in {{ r.engine.old_gap_at_least_100_n }}/{{ r.engine.numeric_n }}. In the same subset, {{ r.engine.played_exact_n }} actual game moves matched the original answer; {{ r.engine.played_within_20_n }} were within 20 centipawns of the best score found by the audited searches.

The sample is small and stratified. Searches use one thread, 128 MB hash, a cleared hash for each search, and a three-second cap. Mate cases are excluded only from centipawn comparisons. Restricted searches can disagree; “best found” is the maximum across the audited candidates, not a proof of optimality. Maia probabilities were not recomputed for changed engine moves, so this is not a fresh mining run.

I tried twelve combinations of the probability and evaluation thresholds on the same data. The selected count changes with those choices. The 4.18% figure belongs to the original filter; it isn’t an estimate of how often these positions occur in chess generally.

Did Leela’s representations improve prediction?

The updated comparison fits the embedding's principal-component transformation using only each training fold. On {{ '{:,}'.format(a._evaluation.n) }} selected positions, the full baseline—surface features, engine evaluations, Maia probabilities, and player rating—scores AUC {{ '%.3f'|format(a['surface + engine + Maia + elo'].auc) }}. Adding 40 embedding components scores {{ '%.3f'|format(a['...+ EMBEDDING'].auc) }}.

I tried two to forty components; none improved on the full baseline. I can’t claim an added predictive benefit from the representations in this comparison. That doesn’t settle what Leela knows: it only tells us what these models could use on this dataset. I also tried the component counts on the same folds, so a firmer comparison needs separate model selection data.

A concept-extraction attempt did not transfer

An earlier experiment fitted sparse directions on Leela rollouts using an adaptation of Schut et al.'s optimisation approach. None of the 30 individually fitted directions generalized under the held-out separation check. Group fitting achieved 1.00 in-group and 0.515 held out. The rollout method, representation and evaluation differed from the AlphaZero study. After that result, I used the representations to organise practice groups. I don’t treat those groups as successfully extracted concepts.

How much to trust the test score

The saved difficulty model reaches AUC {{ '%.3f'|format(d.part_b_difficulty_model['everything cheap'].auc) }} and Brier score {{ '%.3f'|format(d.part_b_difficulty_model._brier) }} across the original corpus, with five folds grouped by game. It predicts exact agreement with the engine in source games. Those data include bot accounts, so this score does not establish performance on human players alone.

The trainer's fixed-rating estimates and test placement use a refit on the full corpus, including trainer positions. Those item curves have not been calibrated against independent participant responses. A website practice session differs from playing under a game clock; the displayed placement is exploratory, not a measured Lichess or FIDE rating.

The 2,000-position pilot

The earlier pilot contains {{ '{:,}'.format(mining_v2.sample.n) }} positions from {{ '{:,}'.format(mining_v2.sample.games) }} games, evenly split between White and Black. Half come from slower club games and half from elite archives. I saved the preceding moves for every position, along with available clocks, and separated source games before selection. Later recovery found that 281 Black pilot states already occurred in the historical corpus; the expanded audit records that earlier development exposure.

This time I’m asking how likely the model is to choose any acceptable move, and how much its likely choices lose. A rare first choice isn’t enough if a common alternative is just as good. Stockfish 18 screens the positions; Maia-3 supplies complete move probabilities with and without history, alongside Maia-2 for comparison.

The first screen shortlisted {{ mining_v2.pilot.screen_candidates_n }} positions under the stated competitive-position, acceptable-move and expected-loss criteria. Deeper checks determine which can become new puzzles. The searches have time limits, and unexamined moves remain explicit in the probability bounds.

Stricter checks retained {{ mining_v2.deep_checks.verified_unique_n }} of {{ mining_v2.deep_checks.unique_positions_n }} reviewed positions, requiring every legal move to reach depths 20 and 24 and the complete acceptable-move set to stay the same. These checks cover new positions and historical teaching candidates.

I also rechecked 64 historical candidates and 64 matched controls. The new screen retained {{ mining_v2.historical.historical_candidate.screen_candidates_n }}/64 candidates and {{ mining_v2.historical.historical_control.screen_candidates_n }}/64 controls. This comparison uses FEN-only policies because those saved rows lack verified move history. It is a selected audit, not an estimate of how common these positions are in all games.

At the fixed 2000 setting, adding history changed Maia-3’s first choice in {{ mining_v2.policy_sensitivity.history_changes_top_move_n }}/{{ mining_v2.sample.n }} positions. That shows why I’m keeping the comparison. It doesn’t establish how often a person would solve the same position when presented as a puzzle.

The club games come from a bounded prefix of June 2026; the elite sample uses September–November 2025 and mixed time controls. Clocks are saved but are not inputs to the current model. Does practicing these positions help?

I haven’t run a controlled learning study yet. The proposed experiment compares grouped and shuffled practice using the same positions, explanations and practice time. Only their order changes, so the comparison can test the value of seeing related examples together. Both groups will take a baseline test, an immediate test on different positions, and a delayed test about a week later. Test positions must come from different games than training positions.

The primary outcome will credit moves within 20 centipawns of an independently verified best score. Exact engine matches, time taken and performance on positive and boundary cases will be secondary outcomes. The proposed 24-person pilot is meant to check whether the study is workable and how uncertain the results are. It may be too small to establish a learning effect.

Read the revised study protocol. The allocation and material-review tools are built. The final private bank still needs stable answers, meaningful counterexamples and independent chess review before recruitment. Ordinary site use is not automatically enrolled in the study.

What I’ve corrected, and what still needs checking

I’ve corrected several claims in earlier versions of the write-up. The full mining run used Maia-2 through 2000; only the smaller Maia-3 experiment reached 2600. A different move isn’t automatically a mistake. Model rankings don’t show what a person was thinking, and variance left unexplained by my motif basis isn’t evidence of unknown chess knowledge.

Earlier difficulty evaluations also had placeholder elite-game IDs; the saved current model results use repaired IDs. Unresolved IDs can still be treated as singleton groups. The new audit found {{ r.duplicate_board_states }} repeated board states after removing move counters, despite full-FEN deduplication. Game grouping does not guarantee separation by player, transposition, opening or time period. Future confirmatory evaluations should address these overlaps.

The feature contrast remains observational; activation-level causal evidence and a controlled learning result are still missing. A separate time-period holdout, matched comparison groups and independently calibrated test items are priorities.
