Can we learn from moves a human model overlooks?

This project finds disagreements between Stockfish and Maia, studies their structure, and turns selected positions into practice. The evidence supports a useful set of difficult examples. Whether practicing them improves play on unseen positions remains an open question.

Research updated 7 September 2026. New checks use the saved corpus and Stockfish 17.1; no new participant study has been completed.

What “machine unique” means here

The term is a selection label, not a claim that no person could find the move. A position qualifies when Stockfish 17.1 at depth 16 gives its chosen move an evaluation at least 100 centipawns above Maia-2's favourite at 2000, and Maia-2 assigns the engine move at most 5% probability at each of 1100, 1400, 1700, and 2000.

This filter selected {{ '{:,}'.format(r.mu_n) }} of {{ '{:,}'.format(r.n) }} positions ({{ '%.2f'|format(r.mu_rate * 100) }}%) across nine mining batches. Sources include Lichess rated-game archives and the Lichess Elite Database, sampled at intervals during games. The corpus balances rating bands, but differs across batches in dates and time controls. It is not a random sample of all chess play. Ratings refer to the source Lichess pools; we do not convert them to FIDE ratings.

The criterion compares the engine move with one model favourite. It does not require a unique good move. In {{ '{:,}'.format(r.near_alternatives[1].n) }} positions ({{ '%.1f'|format(r.near_alternatives[1].rate * 100) }}%), the saved runner-up is less than 20 centipawns behind. Missing the exact engine choice does not necessarily mean making a mistake.

The trainer uses a stricter curated subset: when built, its examples also had to retain the engine move at depth 18 with a runner-up gap of at least 70 centipawns. That extra check does not apply to every position in the research corpus.

A separate Maia-3 experiment reaches 2600

Maia-3 ranked moves on 77 selected positions and 56 controls at five rating settings. On the selected positions, the engine move's top-five inclusion rose from 28.6% at 1100 to 57.1% at 2600; top-one agreement at 2600 was 2.6%. This smaller ranking experiment did not re-filter the full corpus using probabilities through 2600.

Higher top-five inclusion suggests the model ranks the move more favourably at higher ratings. It does not reveal whether a person considered the move and rejected it.

Quiet moves are common, but the comparison is observational

Among positions meeting the same minimum 100-centipawn gap, the low-probability group has more quiet moves and fewer captures or checks. Thirty binary chess features distinguish the low- and high-probability groups at AUC {{ '%.3f'|format(contrast.auc_logistic) }} (five folds grouped by game; 7,853 positions in the two extreme groups). AUC measures ranking discrimination: 0.5 is chance-level ranking and 1 is perfect ranking.

Eight groups for practice

Clustering Leela Chess Zero layer-10 embeddings created eight groups on 1,745 positions. The later 3,410 positions were assigned to their nearest group centre. Together the groups cover all 5,155 selected positions. Eight is a practical teaching choice; the analysis does not identify eight distinct, newly discovered chess concepts.

Inspect the grouping and motif evidence

At eight groups, silhouette is {{ '%.4f'|format(v.silhouette_real['8']) }} against {{ '%.4f'|format(v.silhouette_null['8']) }} for independently shuffled embedding dimensions. Absolute separation is small. This null destroys correlations as well as cluster structure, so the comparison alone does not establish meaningful chess categories.

Six groups are more stable under resampling than eight. Other clustering methods give different partitions and many noise points. The k=8 permutation result, p = {{ '%.3f'|format(v.external_validity['8'].p_value) }}, describes how often the null procedure produced a statistic at least as extreme as observed. It is exploratory across several choices of k, without a multiple-comparison correction.

On an earlier 646-position subset and 400 controls, twelve motif directions reconstructed the average selected-versus-control direction with R² = 0.46. Per-position reconstruction was similar for selected positions and controls (means 0.20 and 0.18). The unexplained component is residual to this particular basis; it is not proof of knowledge beyond chess vocabulary.

What changes when we check more carefully?

Greater engine depth changes some exact answers

A seeded sample of eight positions per rating band, from {{ r.engine.n }} different games, was reanalysed with Stockfish 17.1. All {{ r.engine.complete_n }} reached depth 20 in every search. The original move remained first in {{ r.engine.same_top_n }}/{{ r.engine.n }}.

Of {{ r.engine.numeric_n }} positions without mate scores, the original move still beat Maia-2's 2000 favourite by at least 100 centipawns in {{ r.engine.old_gap_at_least_100_n }}/{{ r.engine.numeric_n }}. In the same subset, {{ r.engine.played_exact_n }} actual game moves matched the original answer; {{ r.engine.played_within_20_n }} were within 20 centipawns of the best score found by the audited searches.

The sample is small and stratified. Searches use one thread, 128 MB hash, a cleared hash for each search, and a three-second cap. Mate cases are excluded only from centipawn comparisons. Restricted searches can disagree; “best found” is the maximum across the audited candidates, not a proof of optimality. Maia probabilities were not recomputed for changed engine moves, so this is not a fresh mining run.

Rerunning the embedding comparison

The updated comparison fits the embedding's principal-component transformation using only each training fold. On {{ '{:,}'.format(a._evaluation.n) }} selected positions, the full baseline—surface features, engine evaluations, Maia probabilities, and player rating—scores AUC {{ '%.3f'|format(a['surface + engine + Maia + elo'].auc) }}. Adding 40 embedding components scores {{ '%.3f'|format(a['...+ EMBEDDING'].auc) }}.

The sweep from two to forty components did not improve on that full baseline. This limits the claim of additional predictive value for these models and this dataset. It does not prove that the representation is redundant or that it contains no additional knowledge. Component counts were explored on the same folds; a confirmatory comparison needs held-out model selection.

A concept-extraction attempt did not transfer

An earlier experiment fitted sparse directions on Leela rollouts using an adaptation of Schut et al.'s optimisation approach. None of the 30 individually fitted directions generalized under our held-out separation check. Group fitting achieved 1.00 in-group and 0.515 held out. The rollout method, representation and evaluation differed from the AlphaZero study. This negative result helps explain the move toward exploratory grouping rather than a claim of successful concept discovery.

Difficulty prediction is not a validated rating test

The saved difficulty model reaches AUC {{ '%.3f'|format(d.part_b_difficulty_model['everything cheap'].auc) }} and Brier score {{ '%.3f'|format(d.part_b_difficulty_model._brier) }} across the full corpus, with five folds grouped by game. It predicts exact agreement with the engine in source games.

The trainer's fixed-rating estimates and test placement use a refit on the full corpus, including trainer positions. Those item curves have not been calibrated against independent participant responses. A website practice session differs from playing under a game clock; the displayed placement is exploratory, not a measured Lichess or FIDE rating.

Does practicing these positions help?

We do not yet have a completed controlled learning study. The next experiment will compare grouped practice with ordinary puzzles matched for difficulty and practice time. Both groups will take a baseline test, an immediate test on different positions, and a delayed test about a week later. Test positions must come from different games than training positions.

The primary outcome will credit moves within 20 centipawns of an independently verified best score. Exact engine matches, time taken and performance on ordinary positions will be secondary outcomes. A proposed 24-person pilot will establish feasibility and uncertainty; it is not a claim of adequate statistical power for a definitive learning effect.

Read the proposed study protocol. It specifies assignment, separate test forms, scoring, exclusions, missing responses and analysis before recruitment. Ordinary site use is not automatically enrolled in the study.

The inspiration is Schut et al., Bridging the Human-AI Knowledge Gap: Concept Discovery and Transfer in AlphaZero. Their human study reports improvements on concept-prototype positions in four grandmasters. This project adapts the idea using Stockfish, Maia and Leela; it has not replicated that learning result.

Corrections and remaining limitations

Earlier copy conflated the Maia-2 mining range with the smaller Maia-3 experiment, treated exact disagreement as error, inferred human thought from model rankings, and interpreted unexplained motif variance as unnamed knowledge. Those claims have been narrowed.

Earlier difficulty evaluations also had placeholder elite-game IDs; the saved current model results use repaired IDs. Unresolved IDs can still be treated as singleton groups. The new audit found {{ r.duplicate_board_states }} repeated board states after removing move counters, despite full-FEN deduplication. Game grouping does not guarantee separation by player, transposition, opening or time period. Future confirmatory evaluations should address these overlaps.

The feature contrast remains observational; activation-level causal evidence and a controlled learning result are still missing. A separate time-period holdout, matched comparison groups and independently calibrated test items are priorities.
