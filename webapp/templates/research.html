How these positions were found

Nothing in this trainer came out of a chess book. It came out of a pipeline that looks for positions where a strong engine is decisively right and essentially no human plays the move. Here is exactly what it does, what it found, and where it falls short.

1 · Finding disagreement

Positions are sampled from public Lichess games — both club play and games between 2300+ rated players. Each is analysed twice: by Stockfish 17.1 at depth 16, and by Maia, a neural network trained to predict what a human of a given rating actually plays rather than what is best.

A position is machine-unique when the engine's move is decisively better — at least 100 centipawns above the human favourite — and Maia gives that move under 5% probability at every rating from 1100 to 2600. Out of 43,603 positions analysed, 1,745 qualified, a rate of 4.0% that held steady across five separate batches and both rating populations.

In 1,499 of those the player who was actually at the board also failed to find the move. In 247, that player was rated 2500 or above.

2 · Blindness, then disbelief

Simulated players agree with the engine more often as they get stronger — on ordinary positions. On machine-unique positions the rate at which they play the engine's move stays near zero from 1100 all the way to 2600. But the rate at which the move appears anywhere in their top five climbs from 29% to 57%.

Weak players never see the move. Strong players see it and reject it.

3 · Grouping them

Each position is embedded using the internal representations of Leela Chess Zero (layer 10), then clustered into eight groups. To characterise each group, its direction is compared against twelve named tactical motifs built from thousands of tagged Lichess puzzles.

The groups are deliberately unnamed. Naming them would be the one move this project cannot justify: a name asserts that the group is a thing chess vocabulary already covers, and the measurements below say that is mostly false. Roughly half of what makes these positions machine-only is expressible in known terms (R² = 0.46) — a signature of sacrifice without a combination to justify it. The other half is not expressible at all. One group, Concept 5, has zero or negative similarity to every named motif.

“Quiet” means the move is neither a capture nor a check. Across all eight groups, between 68% and 94% of these moves are quiet — the part of chess humans cannot see is not tactics. Tactics are what training drills.

4 · Is the grouping real?

Clustering always returns clusters. The only honest thing to do is test whether these ones survive contact with a null hypothesis. Three tests, all of which the grouping could have failed, and one of which it did.

The structure exists. Compared against a control built by shuffling each embedding dimension independently — which destroys the structure while preserving the scale and spectrum of the data — the real embeddings separate roughly nine times better. But the absolute separation is small. These are soft regions in a continuous space, not sharp categories.

The engine's internal representation knows something real about human difficulty that piece, phase and quietness do not: adding it lifts prediction by {{ '%+.3f'|format(e.surface_plus_raw_auc - e.surface_auc) }}. But collapsing that same representation into group labels recovers only {{ '%+.3f'|format(e.by_k['4'].gain_over_surface) }} — roughly a sixth of it. The information lives in the continuous geometry, not in which bucket a position lands in.

So the accurate description of this project is not “eight machine concepts were discovered.” It is: there is a continuous space of positions where engines and humans part company, that space has measurable structure, and eight regions of it are a convenient way to teach from — nothing more. The groups earn their place as a curriculum. They do not earn the status of natural kinds, and they certainly do not earn names.

6 · What this is built on

The approach follows Schut et al., PNAS 2025, who mined concepts from AlphaZero that do not exist in human play and taught them to four grandmasters using only example positions — no explanations, because none were possible. All four improved. This project reproduces the idea on open models and turns the result into something anyone can train on.

Stockfish 17.1 · Maia-2 and Maia-3 (CSSLab, Toronto) · Leela Chess Zero · Lichess open database. Every analysis ran on one laptop.
