How these positions were found

Nothing in this trainer came out of a chess book. It came out of a pipeline that looks for positions where a strong engine is decisively right and essentially no human plays the move. Here is exactly what it does, what it found, and where it falls short.

1 · Finding disagreement

Positions are sampled from public Lichess games — both club play and games between 2300+ rated players. Each is analysed twice: by Stockfish 17.1 at depth 16, and by Maia, a neural network trained to predict what a human of a given rating actually plays rather than what is best.

A position is machine-unique when the engine's move is decisively better — at least 100 centipawns above the human favourite — and Maia gives that move under 5% probability at every rating from 1100 to 2600. Out of 63,486 positions analysed, 2,571 qualified, a rate of 4.0% that has now held steady across six separate batches and both rating populations.

In 2,129 of those the player who was actually at the board also failed to find the move. In 707, that player was rated 2500 or above.

2 · Blindness, then disbelief

Simulated players agree with the engine more often as they get stronger — on ordinary positions. On machine-unique positions the rate at which they play the engine's move stays near zero from 1100 all the way to 2600. But the rate at which the move appears anywhere in their top five climbs from 29% to 57%.

Weak players never see the move. Strong players see it and reject it.

3 · Grouping them

Each position is embedded using the internal representations of Leela Chess Zero (layer 10), then clustered into eight groups. The clusters were built on the first 1,745 qualifying positions; the 826 found by the latest batch have not been assigned to groups, because that requires the embedding model rather than anything in this repository. To characterise each group, its direction is compared against twelve named tactical motifs built from thousands of tagged Lichess puzzles.

The groups are deliberately unnamed. Naming them would be the one move this project cannot justify: a name asserts that the group is a thing chess vocabulary already covers, and the measurements below say that is mostly false. Roughly half of what makes these positions machine-only is expressible in known terms (R² = 0.46) — a signature of sacrifice without a combination to justify it. The other half is not expressible at all. One group, Concept 5, has zero or negative similarity to every named motif.

“Quiet” means the move is neither a capture nor a check. Across all eight groups, between 68% and 94% of these moves are quiet — the part of chess humans cannot see is not tactics. Tactics are what training drills.

4 · Is the grouping real?

Clustering always returns clusters. The only honest thing to do is test whether these ones survive contact with a null hypothesis. Three tests, all of which the grouping could have failed, and one of which it did.

The structure exists. Compared against a control built by shuffling each embedding dimension independently — which destroys the structure while preserving the scale and spectrum of the data — the real embeddings separate roughly nine times better. But the absolute separation is small. These are soft regions in a continuous space, not sharp categories.

5 · What the representation actually adds — a correction

An earlier version of this page claimed the engine's internal representation knows something about human difficulty that ordinary chess features do not. That claim was tested properly and it did not hold. It is left here, corrected, rather than quietly deleted.

The question: given a position, how well can you predict whether the player at the board actually found the move? Scored by area under the ROC curve, where 0.5 is a coin flip, five-fold cross-validation grouped by game so positions from one game never straddle the train/test split.

The first pass compared Leela's embedding against surface chess features — piece, phase, quiet, capture, check, cost — and the embedding won by {{ '%+.3f'|format(e.surface_plus_raw_auc - e.surface_auc) }}. But that baseline had no access to Maia, a network trained specifically to predict human moves, or to the engine's own evaluation. Against a baseline that has both, the picture reverses.

That could have been a sample-size artefact — forty extra features against 246 positive cases can lose on dimensionality alone — so the width was swept from forty components down to two. So the embedding is redundant for this purpose. Whatever it knows about human difficulty, Maia and the engine evaluation already knew. This does not touch section 4 — the clusters still beat the shuffled null, and the k=8 result still holds. What falls is the stronger claim that the representation is a privileged window into what humans cannot see. It is a reasonable way to group positions for teaching. It is not a source of information nothing else has.

6½ · Two audits

The evaluation had a bug, now fixed. Every elite game carried the same placeholder id, so splits that promised “positions from one game never straddle train and test” were treating all {{ '{:,}'.format(29741) }} elite positions as a single game — one fold owned all of them. Real ids were reconstructed by replaying the source archive. With sound folds the fold-to-fold variance fell four-fold and the calibration drift this page previously reported vanished. Numbers on this page are post-repair.

More data no longer helps — except at the top. Holding out {{ '{:,}'.format(lc.test_n) }} positions and training on growing subsets: {% for pt in lc.curve %}{{ '{:,}'.format(pt.train_n) }} → {{ '%.3f'|format(pt.auc_mean) }}{% if not loop.last %}, {% endif %}{% endfor %}. The curve is flat past twenty thousand. But the flatness is a club-position statement — splitting the same held-out set by the rating of the player at the board, a model trained with the second elite batch beats one trained without it by {{ '%+.4f'|format(bv['2600+'].delta_auc) }} AUC at 2600+ and only {{ '%+.4f'|format(bv['<2000'].delta_auc) }} below 2000. The new data helped exactly where it was aimed, and nowhere else.

Every position in this trainer now carries a prediction from that model: how often a 1900-rated player would find the move. It is shown with the feedback after you answer.

7 · What this is built on

The approach follows Schut et al., PNAS 2025, who mined concepts from AlphaZero that do not exist in human play and taught them to four grandmasters using only example positions — no explanations, because none were possible. All four improved. This project reproduces the idea on open models and turns the result into something anyone can train on.

Stockfish 17.1 · Maia-2 and Maia-3 (CSSLab, Toronto) · Leela Chess Zero · Lichess open database. Every analysis ran on one laptop.
